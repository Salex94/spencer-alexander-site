Recovering unpaid debts in Victoria: from letter of demand to statutory demand

Commercial Law 22 Jun 2026 · 8 min read · updated 9 Nov 2026 Published by Spencer Alexander, Principal

Start with a letter of demand, and if that fails, sue and enforce the judgment. A company owing $4,000 or more that it does not genuinely dispute can be served with a statutory demand, which gives it 21 days to pay, secure or compromise the debt or apply to set the demand aside, and if it does none of these, exposes it to a presumption of insolvency for three months.

Why does your paperwork decide the case?

Every recovery runs on the documents that existed when the work was done: the signed terms of trade, the quote, the purchase order, the delivery records and the invoice. Terms that provide for interest on overdue accounts, recovery costs, retention of title and a personal guarantee from the directors of company customers put you in a far stronger position than terms that are silent. If your terms are thin, fix them before the next job, and our guide to the contracts every Victorian business should have explains what belongs in them.

What does a letter of demand achieve?

A letter of demand from a lawyer often resolves a debt without proceedings, or opens the way to a sensible payment plan. It states the debt, its basis, the deadline and what will follow if it is not paid, and it shows the debtor, at little cost, that you are organised and serious. It needs to be firm, precise and accurate, because an inflated or aggressive demand hands the debtor a grievance. Where the debtor has a genuine dispute, the letter often brings it out early, and it is far better to learn of the defence now than after you have paid the court's filing fees. Which court should you sue in?

If the deadline in the letter passes, the next step is proceedings. In Victoria, a debt claim of up to $100,000 is usually brought in the Magistrates' Court, whose website sets out its civil claim forms, fees and procedures, and larger claims go to the County Court or the Supreme Court. Domestic building disputes are different, because VCAT is chiefly responsible for resolving them. Many debt claims end in a default judgment, because the debtor never files a defence, and for a debt the registrar can make that order on the papers. Defended claims go through the court's pre-trial steps, and many settle before a hearing.

How do you enforce a judgment?

A judgment does not pay itself, and if the debtor still does not pay, you have to enforce it. A Magistrates' Court judgment for money can be enforced in these ways.

  • A warrant to seize property authorises the sheriff to seize the debtor's personal property and to sell it if the debt and the costs of execution are not paid.
  • An attachment of earnings order directs the debtor's employer to pay part of the debtor's wages towards the debt.
  • An attachment of debts order, often called a garnishee order, redirects money owed to the debtor, such as a bank balance, to you.
  • A Magistrates' Court judgment can reach land too, once a warrant to seize property has come back wholly or partly unsatisfied, by filing a certificate of the judgment in the Supreme Court and enforcing it there.
  • An oral examination requires the debtor to come to court and answer questions on oath about their income, assets and debts, usually on an application for an order that the judgment be paid by instalments, and the summons alone sometimes produces payment.

The right step depends on what you know about the debtor's assets. An attachment of earnings suits a debtor who is employed, and an attachment of debts suits a company with money moving through its bank account, timed for when its own customers usually pay it.

When can you serve a statutory demand on a company?

You can serve one when the debtor is a company and the debt is $4,000 or more, due and payable and not genuinely disputed. Served under the Corporations Act, a statutory demand gives the company 21 days to pay the debt, secure it or reach a compromise to the creditor's reasonable satisfaction, or apply to set the demand aside. An application to set the demand aside can only be made within those 21 days, and only if, within that time, the supporting affidavit is filed with the court and copies of the application and the affidavit are served on the creditor. If the company does none of these, a court hearing a winding up application made within the following three months must presume that it is insolvent unless the contrary is proved.

Directors take that risk seriously, which is why a demand that complies with the Act so often produces payment, although a company in genuine difficulty may instead turn to the protections explained in our guide to safe harbour and small business restructuring. Creditors need care too. A demand served for a debt that is genuinely disputed is likely to be set aside, or reduced to the part that is not in dispute, and the creditor may be ordered to pay the company's costs, so a statutory demand suits only debts that are clearly owed.

When can you serve a bankruptcy notice on an individual?

Once you hold a final judgment against the individual for $10,000 or more, you can apply for a bankruptcy notice and serve it on them. If the debtor does not comply with the bankruptcy notice within the time it allows, that failure is an act of bankruptcy on which a creditor's petition can be based. The threat is a serious one, because bankruptcy reaches homes and livelihoods, but weigh the economics carefully, because once the debtor is bankrupt, the debts proved in the bankruptcy rank equally and you may have to share what is recovered with every other creditor.

How quickly should you act on an unpaid invoice?

Act as soon as an invoice is overdue, and follow a fixed timetable: a reminder at 7 days, a call to a director at 14, a lawyer's letter of demand at 21, and proceedings or a statutory demand on a date you set and keep. Recovery prospects fall with every month an invoice ages, because the debtor's position worsens, other creditors move first, and your bargaining power as a continuing supplier disappears once the relationship ends. Creditors who keep to a timetable generally recover more than those who chase in occasional bursts.

Watch for the warning signs of insolvency while you wait, such as round-sum part payments, changed bank details, a new entity trading from the same premises or directors who are suddenly unreachable. Any of these accelerates the timetable. If a liquidation does come, a retention of title registered on the PPSR within 20 business days of the customer accepting your terms is often the difference between secured recovery and an unsecured dividend. A director's guarantee works differently. It is not security over the company's property, but it gives you a claim against the director personally, which you can pursue separately from the liquidation.

When does pursuing a debt make commercial sense?

Pursuing a debt makes sense when the debt is clear, the debtor can pay and each step costs less than it is realistically likely to recover, and that is the question to ask before every step. Recovering a disputed debt is estimated in writing once we know what is involved. In the Magistrates' Court, the costs a losing debtor is ordered to pay are fixed on the court's scale and usually cover only part of what you have spent, and a judgment against a debtor with nothing to take costs you money rather than recovering it. Sometimes the better commercial answer is an instalment plan backed by security, or accepting 90 cents in the dollar now rather than pursuing the full amount and recovering nothing.

Prevention matters as much. Credit checks on new customers, deposits, a retention of title registered on the PPSR and guarantees from the directors of company customers all reduce the debts you will be chasing next year, and our commercial law page explains how we act on disputes and debt recovery.

When you first call, have the essentials to hand: the contract or terms the customer agreed to, the invoices and statements, any written promise to pay, and a note of every conversation about the debt. That record usually decides which step comes next, whether a firm demand will do, whether the amount belongs in the Magistrates' Court or a higher court, or whether a statutory demand is available at all. With that record in front of us, the first call can turn straight to what happens next rather than what is missing.

How do you recover debts reliably?

Reliable recovery comes from terms of trade agreed before the work, a fixed timetable for chasing every overdue account, and a decision at each step about whether the next one is worth its cost. Once that is in place, the next unpaid invoice is handled as routine rather than as an emergency.

Common questions

Will chasing the debt cost more than the debt?

Sometimes. In the Magistrates' Court, the costs a losing debtor is ordered to pay are fixed on the court's scale and usually cover only part of what you spend, so a claim is worth pursuing when the debt is clear, the debtor can pay and each step is in proportion to the amount. Interest and recovery cost clauses in your terms of trade can shift more of that cost to the debtor.

The debtor says they will pay "when cash flow improves". Do I wait?

No, not on a promise alone. Ask for a signed payment plan that says what happens if an instalment is missed, for security, or for payments committed to fixed dates, and if the debtor will not put the promise in writing, treat that refusal as your answer.
