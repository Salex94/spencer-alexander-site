Choosing a business structure in Victoria: sole trader, partnership, company or trust

Commercial Law 6 Apr 2026 · 8 min read · updated 9 Nov 2026 Published by Spencer Alexander, Principal

The right business structure in Victoria depends on what can go wrong, who shares the profits, whether investors will ever come in and what happens on sale. A sole trader has unlimited personal liability, partners are jointly liable, a company generally limits each shareholder's liability to any amount unpaid on their shares, and a discretionary trust offers flexible distribution of income and a layer of asset protection.

When does trading as a sole trader make sense?

Trading as a sole trader makes sense for a low risk service business that is still testing an idea, and it becomes uncomfortable the day you hire staff, sign a lease or supply anything that could fail expensively. The business is you, trading under an ABN and perhaps a registered business name, so it costs almost nothing to set up. The cost of that simplicity is unlimited personal liability, because every debt of the business and every claim against it is yours and can reach your house and your savings. The profit is also your own income, and there is no separate entity in which profit can be kept or shared with family members.

What are the risks of a partnership?

The main risk of a partnership is that the partners share liability for everything the business owes. A partnership arises when people carry on a business in common with a view of profit, in Victoria under the Partnership Act 1958 (Vic). It is cheap and flexible, and it pays no tax itself, because each partner's share of the net income is included in that partner's own assessable income. However, each partner can bind the firm when acting in the usual way of its business, and each is liable, jointly with the others, for the debts of the firm, including debts another partner incurred for it.

A written partnership agreement covering decisions, money, disputes and exits is essential, because it settles in advance what happens when the partners disagree or one of them wants to leave. Many groups that begin as partnerships become companies or trusts once the business has real value.

What does a company give you, and what does it ask of you?

A proprietary limited company is a separate legal person from the day it is registered. It owns the assets, signs the contracts and employs the staff, and a shareholder's liability for its debts is generally limited to any amount unpaid on their shares. A company pays tax on its own profits at the company rate, which for most trading small businesses is lower than the top personal rates, so profit can be kept in the company and reinvested. Ownership is divided into shares, which is what makes outside investors, employee equity and staged sales possible.

A company also asks things of you. It must keep the registers and financial records the Corporations Act requires. Its directors owe duties that carry personal consequences, among them the duty to act with care and diligence, the duty to act in good faith in the company's best interests and for a proper purpose, and the duty to prevent the company incurring debts while it is insolvent. Every director needs a director identification number and must generally apply for one before being appointed. Limited liability also has practical limits, because landlords and banks routinely ask the directors of small companies for personal guarantees, and unless its terms are negotiated, a guarantee puts your home within reach of the landlord or the bank if the company does not pay. When does a trust suit a business?

A trust suits a family business that wants flexibility over who receives its income each year, and it suits holding assets better than raising outside money. A discretionary trust, often called a family trust, is not a separate legal person. A trustee, often a company, holds the business or the investments for a group of family beneficiaries and decides each year how the income is distributed among them. Its attractions are that flexibility and a layer of asset protection, because a beneficiary of a discretionary trust has no fixed entitlement to the trust's property until the trustee decides to distribute to them.

A trust usually distributes its income every year, because income left undistributed is generally taxed in the trustee's hands at the top marginal rate. Lenders often ask more questions before lending to a trust, and outside investors rarely invest through a discretionary trust, because it gives them no fixed share. A unit trust, in which each holder has a fixed share, suits some joint ventures.

How do structures combine as a business grows?

As a business grows, its structure usually becomes a combination of entities. Common examples are a trading company owned by a family trust, premises or intellectual property held in a separate entity and licensed to the trading business, and a company with no significant assets of its own acting as trustee in place of individuals. The principle is to keep valuable assets away from the entity that carries the trading risk, and getting that right needs legal and tax advice given together, so we work alongside your accountant from the start.

How do you choose the right structure?

Five considerations usually decide the choice.

  • The more that can go wrong in the business, and the more it would cost, the sooner a company makes sense.
  • Where profits are shared within a family with different incomes, a trust is often worth considering, while a founder who wants investors usually needs a company.
  • Investors buy shares, so if outside money may come in, choose a structure for where the business is going as well as where it starts.
  • The structure affects both the tax on your exit and how cleanly a buyer can acquire the business, so think about a sale from the beginning.
  • Simplicity sometimes costs nothing, and a low risk consultant may sensibly stay a sole trader for years.

Can you change structure once you are trading?

Yes, and most businesses outgrow their first structure. Moving from a sole trader or a partnership into a company or a trust is routine work. A small business that moves its business assets into a new structure may be able to choose a roll-over under which the tax law disregards the gains and losses the transfer would otherwise produce, provided the move is a genuine restructure of an ongoing business that does not change who ultimately owns the assets. Your accountant will confirm whether it applies before anything moves.

The real risk is drift, where a grown business keeps carrying its risks on the structure it started with because changing it felt like paperwork. If nobody has reviewed your structure since the business reached its current size, a review is due.

Is there one right structure?

No. The right structure is the one that fits this business, the family behind it and the risks it carries, and it should be reviewed whenever any of those changes. Choose it deliberately at the start, look at it again each time the business grows, and weigh the modest cost of restructuring against the personal exposure it removes.

Common questions

Can I just start as a sole trader and fix it later?

Often, yes. Starting as a sole trader is sensible for a low risk venture, and the business can move into a company or a trust later. The mistake is staying a sole trader after you hire staff, sign a lease or take on real commercial risk.

Does a company protect me completely?

No. A company limits your exposure, but as a director you can still be ordered to pay compensation for debts the company incurs while it is insolvent, you can be made to pay penalties equal to some of its unpaid tax and superannuation guarantee charge, and you remain liable under anything you have personally guaranteed.

Should my family business use a trust or a company?

It usually depends on who takes the profits and how stable those profits are. Flexible distributions among family members favour a trust, while retained earnings, outside investors and employee share plans favour a company. The decision is yours, and it is best made on advice from your lawyer and your accountant together.
