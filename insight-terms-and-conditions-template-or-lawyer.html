Can I use a template for my terms and conditions, or do I need a lawyer?

Commercial Law 26 Oct 2026 · 7 min read By Spencer Alexander, Principal

A template can be a reasonable start for a small business that sells simply, but it is rarely safe to trade on one unchanged. Most templates are not written for the way you actually trade, some contain clauses the Australian Consumer Law makes void, and a retention of title clause will rarely protect you in a customer's insolvency unless it is properly documented and registered on the PPSR.

When is a template a reasonable start?

A template is not unlawful, and no law requires a business to have a lawyer draft its terms. For a sole trader selling a simple service to a few regular business customers, a well made template that the owner reads closely and adapts can be a sensible first step, and it is far better than trading on no written terms at all.

The difficulty is that a template is written for nobody in particular. It cannot know whether you sell goods, services or both, whether your customers are households or businesses, whether you give credit, how your quotes become orders, or what tends to go wrong in your industry. Each of those changes what the terms need to say, and some of them change what the law allows the terms to say. The risk grows with the value of each sale, the amount of credit you extend and the share of your customers who are consumers.

Why must your terms match how you actually trade?

As a rule, terms bind a customer only if the customer agreed to them before the contract was made. Terms that sit somewhere on your website, or are printed on the back of an invoice sent after the work is done, may never become part of the contract at all. How the terms are put in front of the customer, and how the customer accepts them, has to fit your real process, whether that is a signed quote, an online checkout, a credit application or the customer's own purchase order.

Templates found online are often written for a different kind of business, and sometimes for another country, so they refer to the wrong law and the wrong courts or set payment and delivery rules the owner has never followed. A court reads a contract as it is written. Where the document says one thing and both parties have always done another, there is room for an argument about what the contract really was, and that argument usually arrives at the moment a customer owes you money.

What can a template get wrong under the Australian Consumer Law?

The most common fault is a clause that tries to exclude the consumer guarantees. The Australian Consumer Law, which is Schedule 2 to the Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth), gives consumers guarantees that apply automatically, for example that goods are of acceptable quality and that services are provided with due care and skill. A term is void to the extent that it tries to exclude, restrict or modify those guarantees. A clause saying all warranties are excluded, or that no refunds are given, is worse than useless, because a false or misleading representation about the existence, exclusion or effect of a guarantee, right or remedy is itself a contravention of the Act.

The Act defines a consumer more widely than most owners expect. Subject to some exceptions, such as goods bought to resell, it covers anyone acquiring goods or services of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal, domestic or household use, and anyone whose purchase costs no more than a threshold amount the law sets, so many business customers are consumers too. Where goods or services are not of a kind ordinarily acquired for personal or household use, a supplier may limit its liability for most failures to repair, replacement or supplying the services again, or the cost of doing so, unless the customer shows that relying on the limit is not fair or reasonable. A template rarely draws that line correctly for your business.

If your terms promise to repair, replace or refund defective goods or services, that promise is a warranty against defects. The Competition and Consumer Regulations 2010 (Cth) set out what a warranty against defects given to a consumer must contain, including the business's name, address and telephone number, how to make a claim, who bears the cost of claiming, and set wording explaining that the consumer guarantees cannot be excluded. Giving a consumer a warranty document that does not comply can attract a penalty.

Will a template retention of title clause protect you?

Many templates include a retention of title clause, which says you keep ownership of the goods until you are paid. Under the Personal Property Securities Act 2009 (Cth), that clause gives you a security interest in the goods. To be enforceable against anyone other than the customer, it needs writing that the customer signed or accepted and that describes the goods, and once the goods have left your hands it generally needs a registration on the Personal Property Securities Register to be perfected. If the customer goes into liquidation, voluntary administration or bankruptcy while your interest is unperfected, the interest vests in the customer, which usually leaves you as an unsecured creditor with no claim to the goods.

A template can contain the clause, but it cannot register anything, and it cannot tell you whether your sales process produces the signed or accepted writing the Act requires. This is where the gap between a template and properly set up terms costs the most, because it shows only when a customer fails while owing you money. Could a template expose you to an unfair terms penalty?

Terms of trade are usually a standard form contract, and if one party to a proceeding alleges that a contract is in standard form, the law presumes it is unless the other party proves otherwise. Where the customer is a consumer, or either party is a small business, an unfair term in a standard form contract is void. A small business for this purpose includes one employing fewer than 100 people, or one whose turnover in its last income year was under $10 million, so the rules reach a great many businesses and their customers. Proposing an unfair term, or relying on one, can also attract a pecuniary penalty, and each unfair term proposed is a separate contravention.

A term is unfair if it would cause a significant imbalance in the parties' rights and obligations, is not reasonably necessary to protect the legitimate interests of the business it favours, and would cause the customer detriment if it were relied on. The Act gives examples, among them terms that allow only one side to terminate, to vary the contract or to limit its own performance, and terms that penalise only one side for a breach. Those one sided clauses are exactly what templates written to protect the seller tend to contain. A template can therefore be harder on your customers than the law allows, and the clause you were counting on fails when you need it.

Common questions

Can I copy another business's terms and conditions?

It is a poor idea. They were written for another business and its way of trading, so they will not match yours, and any fault in them under the Australian Consumer Law or the unfair terms rules becomes yours once you use them.

Do my terms of trade need to be signed?

Not always, but the customer generally must agree to them before the contract is made, and a retention of title clause depends on writing the customer signed or accepted. A signed credit application, or an online checkout the customer must accept, is the simplest way to show both.

Can my terms say that no refunds are given?

Not where the consumer guarantees apply. A term cannot exclude them, and telling a consumer they have no right to a refund when the guarantees give one can be a false or misleading representation under the Australian Consumer Law.
