My ex will not comply with our property orders. What can I do?

Family Law 19 Oct 2026 · 7 min read By Spencer Alexander, Principal

Ask the court to enforce them. Final property orders bind your former partner whether or not they cooperate, and the court can carry them out without their help. It can appoint someone to sign a transfer in their name, issue a warrant to seize and sell their property, direct their employer or bank to pay you, and order them to pay the costs of making them comply.

Are property orders binding if my ex ignores them?

Yes. Final property orders, whether made after a trial or by consent, bind both of you, and refusing to sign, pay or move out does not undo them. The Family Law Act 1975 (Cth) provides that orders made under it can be enforced by a court with jurisdiction under the Act, and the court's family law rules set out how. Those rules treat four kinds of obligation as enforceable: paying money, signing a document, giving up possession of land, and transferring or handing over other property, together with the costs of enforcing them.

What the court will not do is enforce something the orders do not say. Enforcement gives effect to the orders as they were made, so the first question is always exactly what they require, of whom and by when. Orders that fix a date for each step are far easier to enforce than orders that leave the timing open.

What should I do before going back to court?

Work out precisely which steps have been missed, then put it in writing. A short letter to your former partner, or to their lawyer, that identifies the order, the missed step and a reasonable date to fix it often resolves the matter. If it does not, the letter is evidence that they knew what was required.

Keep the sealed orders and every letter, message and statement about compliance. Where money is owed and you do not know what your former partner now has, the rules let you give them a written notice requiring a Financial Statement within 14 days, before you apply for an enforcement order, so that you know which assets and income there are to enforce against.

What if they will not sign the transfer?

Section 106A of the Act deals with this directly. Where an order directs a person to sign a deed or instrument and they refuse or neglect to do so, the court can appoint an officer of the court or another person to sign it in their name and to do whatever else is needed to make it effective. A document signed that way has the same force as if your former partner had signed it. The rules allow the application to be made without notice to the other party, by filing an enforcement application with an affidavit.

So the transfer can be completed without your former partner's cooperation. If they have instead tried to sell, mortgage or give away property the orders deal with, section 106B gives the court power to set aside or restrain a transaction made to defeat an order, or likely to defeat one, and that needs to be raised quickly.

What if they will not pay what was ordered?

Where the orders require a lump sum and it has not been paid, the rules provide four kinds of enforcement order, and more than one can be sought. An Enforcement Warrant authorises an enforcement officer to seize and sell your former partner's real or personal property to pay what is owed, and stays in force for 12 months from its issue. A Third Party Debt Notice attaches money that someone else owes them, such as funds in a bank account or their wages, so that the bank or the employer pays it to you directly, although an employer must leave them with at least the protected earnings rate. The court can also appoint a sequestrator to take control of their property, or a receiver of their income or property, where the court is satisfied that suits the case.

If you do not know enough about their finances, you can require them to attend an enforcement hearing. They must serve a Financial Statement on you at least seven days beforehand, attend, answer questions about their finances and bring the documents you have asked for.

An unpaid sum also grows. Unless the court orders otherwise, interest runs on money a property order requires to be paid, at the rate the court's rules prescribe, from the date of the order or the date it takes effect, whichever is later. Periodic maintenance payments are treated differently.

What is the National Enforcement List?

From 21 September 2026, the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia has sent enforcement applications in financial and property matters to a dedicated National Enforcement List, under a practice direction made for that purpose. Its aims include securing compliance and sending suitable matters to dispute resolution. Applications are listed for a first return hearing unless an urgent hearing has been granted, and the practice direction warns that a respondent who makes enforcement necessary may be ordered to pay the applicant's legal costs.

The list hears enforcement applications, which ask the court to carry out what was ordered. It does not hear contravention or contempt applications, which ask the court to punish a person for breaching an order and are a separate step. Under Part XIIIA of the Act the court can impose sanctions, such as a bond or a fine, on a person who has contravened a property order without reasonable excuse. A sanction does not by itself put the house in your name, however, so where the aim is to complete the settlement, enforcement is usually the first step.

Can the orders be changed if they cannot be carried out?

Sometimes enforcement is not the answer, because the lender will not refinance or the default itself has changed what is fair. Section 79A of the Act, and section 90SN for de facto partners, allow the court to vary or set aside property orders and make new ones in limited circumstances, and two of them matter here. The first is where circumstances that have arisen since the orders were made mean the orders, or part of them, cannot practicably be carried out. The second is where a person has defaulted on an obligation under the orders and, because of what the default has caused, it is just and equitable to vary them or replace them.

Both are discretionary, and the court must protect the interests of a buyer in good faith or anyone else with an interest in the property. Whether to enforce the orders or ask the court to change them is a judgment best made early.

Who pays the costs of enforcement?

The starting point under the Act is that each party pays their own costs. The court can depart from that where the circumstances justify it, and one of the matters it must consider is whether the proceedings were made necessary by a party's failure to comply with earlier orders. The rules also treat the costs of enforcement as part of what can be enforced, so an enforcement order states the total to be paid, including the amount owed, any interest and the costs of enforcing.

None of that guarantees you will recover everything you spend, so the cost of enforcing is weighed against what is owed before an application is filed. When we act, you receive a written fee estimate before any substantive work begins.

Common questions

Can someone else sign the transfer if my former partner refuses?

Yes. Where orders direct a person to sign a document and they refuse or neglect to, the court can appoint an officer of the court or another person to sign it in their name, with the same effect as their own signature.

Does interest run on a property settlement payment that is late?

Generally yes. Unless the court orders otherwise, interest runs on money a property order requires to be paid, at the rate the court's rules prescribe, from the date of the order or the date it takes effect, whichever is later.

Will my former partner have to pay my enforcement costs?

Possibly. Each party usually pays their own costs, but the court can order otherwise and must consider whether the proceedings were made necessary by a failure to comply with earlier orders.

Is an enforcement application the same as a contravention application?

No. An enforcement application asks the court to carry out what was ordered, and in financial and property matters it goes to the National Enforcement List. A contravention application asks the court to impose a sanction for breaching an order, and is a separate step.
