Separation in Victoria: your first 30 days

Family Law Free checklist · 18 Aug 2026 By Spencer Alexander, Principal

Separation rarely arrives on a schedule, and the first month is when the most consequential decisions tend to get made with the least clarity. This checklist sets out, roughly in the order most people need it, the practical steps worth taking in the first thirty days after a relationship ends in Victoria. It is a starting point, not a substitute for advice on your own circumstances.

Right now: if there is any danger

Safety comes before every other item on this list.

  • If you or your children are in immediate danger, call 000. Nothing else on this page comes before that.
  • If there is a risk of family violence, put together a safety plan: somewhere you could go, a bag ready, and a trusted person who knows what is happening.
  • Know that a family violence intervention order can be sought urgently in Victoria, including before the other person is told. Our guide to intervention orders in Victoria explains how the process works.
  • Tell someone you trust what is going on. Managing a separation alone, on top of everything else, makes every later decision harder.

In the first few days: the practical basics

Separation does not need a form, a court order, or even both people to agree. It begins once one person communicates, in words or by action, that the relationship is over, and it can happen while a couple still shares a home, known as separation under one roof. What matters in the early days is protecting your position while things are still unsettled.

  • Note the date you consider the relationship to have ended, and write down why, in case it is needed later.
  • Gather copies of key documents while access is easy: identification, superannuation statements, tax returns, property titles and loan documents.
  • Avoid signing anything new or agreeing to anything permanent under pressure in the first few days.
  • Change passwords you no longer want a former partner to access, and do not access theirs.

This week: protect your financial position

Joint finances often need attention early, even where the separation itself is amicable.

  • List what you own and owe, together and separately: bank accounts, property, superannuation, shares, business interests and debts.
  • Get advice before moving significant money out of a joint account. Acting unilaterally can escalate a dispute that might otherwise have been resolved calmly.
  • Check whether Centrelink and family assistance payments need to be updated to reflect your new circumstances.
  • Keep paying joint bills and loan repayments where you reasonably can. Missed payments and defaults tend to become a shared problem later, regardless of who was responsible.

This week: if there are children involved

Children generally cope best when as much stays familiar as possible while the adults work things out.

  • Try to keep routines, school and each parent's relationship with the children as stable as circumstances allow.
  • An informal arrangement that works for now does not lock anything in permanently later.
  • Keep children out of adult decisions, and avoid using them to pass messages between parents.
  • Where an informal arrangement cannot be agreed, family dispute resolution is usually the next step before any court application.

Read our guide to parenting arrangements after separation for how the law approaches these decisions.

Weeks two to four: get advice before you decide anything permanent

Even an amicable separation runs on a clock, and the earlier you understand your position, the more options stay open.

  • Get advice on where you stand before agreeing to anything final about property, parenting or support.
  • Understand that strict time limits apply to bring a property or spousal maintenance claim, generally twelve months from divorce for married couples and two years from separation for de facto couples. Feeling amicable now does not remove the deadline later.
  • If you were married, remember that divorce and property settlement are separate processes with separate rules. Our guide to getting divorced in Victoria explains the twelve month separation requirement.
  • If you were in a de facto relationship, check whether you meet the threshold to bring a claim at all. Our guide to de facto separation in Victoria covers the gateway tests.

Weeks two to four: put arrangements in writing where you can

Informal arrangements are a reasonable starting point, but they carry limits worth understanding.

  • A simple written parenting plan reduces confusion, even though it does not carry the same legal weight as court orders.
  • An informal agreement about property is not binding until it is formalised, so avoid treating a verbal understanding as final.
  • Keep a written record of what has been agreed and when, since memories of informal conversations tend to differ later.

Common questions

Do we need to formally announce or register our separation? No. Separation happens through your actions and communication, not paperwork. If you were married and later apply for divorce, you will need to show twelve months of separation, which is easier if you can point to a clear date you both agree on.

Can we still be separated while living in the same house? Yes. The law recognises separation under one roof, but you generally need to show the relationship itself ended: separate finances and bedrooms, and family or friends told. Keep a note of when and how this happened in case it is needed later.

Does it matter who moves out of the family home? Leaving the family home does not, on its own, forfeit your legal entitlement to a share of the property. But the decision affects children's stability and how negotiations unfold, so get advice before deciding, particularly where a business or family violence is involved.

How soon should I speak to a lawyer after separating? Earlier than most people expect, ideally within the first few weeks. Advice does not have to lead to a dispute. It means you understand your options and any deadlines before decisions become difficult to change.
