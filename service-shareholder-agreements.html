Who we act for

When owners call us.

We act for founders starting a company together, families bringing the next generation or an investor into the business, owners whose company was set up years ago with nothing agreed between them, and co-owners who have started to disagree. How we help

What we do for owners.

Setting up a company

We register a proprietary company with ASIC, with its constitution, consents, register of members, share certificates and first resolutions, and an ABN and GST registration if needed, working alongside your accountant so the legal and tax positions pull in the same direction. The company exists from the start of the day it is registered.

Shareholder agreements

We draft the agreement around what the owners have agreed: which decisions need everyone's consent, how shares can be sold or transferred, what breaks a deadlock, how an owner leaves or is bought out and at what value, any restraints, and how disputes are resolved.

Constitutions that match

Without a constitution of its own, a company's internal management is generally governed by the Act's replaceable rules. Under them, for example, the directors of a proprietary company can refuse to register a share transfer for any reason, and new shares must be offered first to existing holders of that class in proportion to their holdings unless the members resolve otherwise. We draft the constitution and the agreement to work together.

When co-owners fall out

Where a company's affairs are conducted against the interests of the members as a whole, or in a way that is oppressive, unfairly prejudicial or unfairly discriminatory to a member, the court can make a wide range of orders, including that one owner buy the other's shares or that the company be wound up. We act for owners on either side, and a good agreement sets out a way through a deadlock before it comes to that.

The agreement and the constitution

How the two documents work together.

The constitution binds the company and every member, and can be changed by special resolution. A shareholder agreement is a contract, so it binds only those who sign it, and it cannot change the constitution by itself. A well drafted agreement usually provides that it prevails between the shareholders who sign it, and obliges them to change the constitution to remove any conflict.

Equal owners need this most. When two owners each hold half the shares, neither can outvote the other, and decisions that need a majority of the directors or the shareholders can stall entirely, so the agreement should say what happens next, often a process for one owner to buy the other out at a value worked out in an agreed way.

Good to know

Common questions about shareholder agreements.

Do we need a shareholder agreement if the company has a constitution?

Usually, yes. The constitution sets the company's internal rules and binds every member, but it is commonly a standard document that says little about what the owners have agreed between themselves, such as what happens in a deadlock or when one of them wants to leave. A shareholder agreement deals with those questions in detail and binds the shareholders who sign it.

Does a shareholder agreement override the constitution?

Not by itself. As a contract, it binds only those who sign it, and it cannot change the constitution, which needs a special resolution passed by at least 75 per cent of the votes cast. A well drafted agreement usually provides that it prevails between the parties and obliges them to change the constitution to remove any conflict.

What happens if co-owners fall out and there is no agreement?

The constitution and the Corporations Act decide, and equal owners can find that neither can outvote the other. If the dispute cannot be negotiated, the court can make orders where the company's affairs are conducted oppressively or unfairly against a member, or against the interests of the members as a whole, including that one owner buy the other's shares, and it can order the company to be wound up where that is just and equitable.

How many directors and shareholders does a company need?

A proprietary company needs at least one member and at least one director who ordinarily resides in Australia, and every director must be at least 18. It does not need a company secretary, although it can have one, and it can have no more than 50 shareholders who are not employees. Every director also needs a director identification number.
