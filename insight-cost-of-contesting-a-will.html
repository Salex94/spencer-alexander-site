What does it cost to contest a will in Victoria, and who pays?

Wills & Estates 23 Nov 2026 · 8 min read Published by Spencer Alexander, Principal

Contesting a will in Victoria costs more the further the dispute goes, and most claims settle before a trial, often at mediation. The court decides who pays, unless the parties agree it in a settlement, and the estate does not always bear the costs. A family provision claim must generally be made within six months of the grant of probate or letters of administration.

What decides how much a will dispute costs?

The biggest factor is the stage at which the dispute ends. A claim that resolves after an exchange of letters costs a fraction of one that is prepared for trial, and most of the cost of a will dispute comes from preparing for and running a trial, including the affidavits, the financial evidence, counsel's fees and the days in court. Most family provision claims settle well before that point, often at a mediation, where the parties and their lawyers spend a day with a mediator working towards terms.

Three other things move the figure. The first is how much of the history is disputed, because a claim in which everyone accepts the facts of the family is far cheaper than one in which the claimant's relationship with the deceased is contested at every turn. The second is the number of people taking part, since each beneficiary who joins in brings their own evidence and argument. The third is the estate itself, and whether assets such as a business, a family trust or a farm need to be valued or untangled before anyone can say what a fair outcome looks like.

What is said at a mediation is also protected. Where the Supreme Court refers a proceeding to mediation, nothing said or done at the mediation can be admitted in evidence at the hearing unless all the parties who attend agree in writing, so the parties can discuss what would genuinely resolve the claim without that discussion being used against them later.

Who pays the legal costs when a will is contested?

A family provision claim is brought in the Supreme Court or the County Court, and in both courts the costs of a proceeding are in the court's discretion. The court decides who pays and to what extent, and it is not bound to order that costs come out of the estate. A claimant whose claim succeeds will often have some or all of their costs paid from the estate, but a claimant who loses, or who presses a claim well beyond what their circumstances justify, can be ordered to pay costs personally, including the costs of the other side.

Even an order in your favour does not necessarily cover everything you have paid your own lawyer. The costs you recover and the costs you incur can be two quite different numbers, and the gap between them is worth asking about at the start.

Most claims never reach a costs order, because they settle, and the costs then become part of the settlement. The terms usually deal with them expressly, for example by stating an amount for the claimant that includes costs, or by providing that each party pays its own.

Does the executor's legal bill come out of the estate?

An executor who defends a claim is generally entitled to have their properly incurred costs paid from the estate. The Trustee Act 1958 allows a trustee, a term that extends to an executor, to reimburse themselves from the estate for expenses incurred in carrying out the trusts and powers. That entitlement depends on the executor acting properly. An executor who takes sides between family members, or defends the will for their own benefit rather than the estate's, risks the court refusing to let those costs come from the estate, or ordering them to bear costs personally.

Beneficiaries feel both sides of the bill. Every dollar the estate spends on the dispute is a dollar they do not receive, and unless the court orders otherwise, any provision it makes for a claimant is borne by the beneficiaries in proportion to the value of their interests in the estate. A beneficiary whose gift is under threat often has as much reason as anyone to want an early, sensible settlement.

How long do you have, and why does timing matter?

A family provision claim must generally be made within six months after the date of the grant of probate or letters of administration. The court can extend that time, even after it has expired, but only on an application made before the estate is finally distributed, and an extension does not disturb anything distributed before the application was made.

The same six months govern the executor. Once they have passed, an executor who has had no notice of a claim is generally protected if they properly distribute the estate. If the executor receives a written notice of an intention to make a claim, signed by the eligible person or their lawyer, that protection returns once three months pass without notice that an application has been filed, and the notice cannot be renewed. A claimant who leaves the first call until the last few weeks has less time to gather evidence and negotiate before the claim must be filed, and fewer choices about how to run it.

Is no win, no fee available for a will dispute?

The Legal Profession Uniform Law allows a conditional costs agreement, under which some or all of the legal costs are payable only if the matter ends in a successful outcome, and some firms offer one in will disputes. The law sets firm conditions on it. The agreement must be in writing and in plain language, set out what counts as a successful outcome, be signed by the client, state that the client has been told of the right to seek independent legal advice, and allow a cooling-off period of at least five clear business days. It may also require disbursements, such as court fees and the fees of experts, to be paid whatever the outcome.

Two further limits protect the client. In a litigious matter, an uplift fee, which is an additional amount charged for a successful outcome, may be included only where the lawyer reasonably believes a successful outcome is reasonably likely, and it cannot exceed 25 per cent of the legal costs, excluding disbursements, otherwise payable. A costs agreement also cannot calculate the lawyer's fee by reference to the amount of any award or settlement, so a fee taken as a percentage of what you recover is prohibited.

Whatever the agreement says, it governs only what you pay your own lawyer. It does not answer for a costs order the court makes against you in favour of another party, and the risk of an adverse order belongs in the first conversation about any claim.

What should a lawyer tell you about costs before you engage them?

Unless the costs are likely to be modest, a law practice must give you in writing, when or as soon as practicable after you first give instructions, the basis on which its costs will be calculated and an estimate of the total legal costs. It must also tell you of your rights to negotiate the costs agreement and the billing method, to receive a bill and ask for an itemised one, and to seek the regulator's help if there is a dispute about costs. If anything it has told you changes significantly, including the costs you are likely to pay, it must tell you that in writing as well.

The costs themselves must be fair and reasonable, both proportionately and reasonably incurred and proportionate and reasonable in amount, and a law practice must not act in a way that unnecessarily increases them. In a will dispute, a sensible budget weighs the costs on every side against the size of the estate and what the claim can realistically achieve.

What will a lawyer look at first?

The first questions are practical. A lawyer will want to know whether a grant has been made and on what date, whether you are an eligible person under the Act, how large the estate is and who else takes from it. With those answers, they can tell you whether a claim is open to you, how much of the six months is left, and whether the amount realistically at stake justifies the cost of pursuing it, which for a modest estate is often the deciding question.

It helps to have the will, the grant if there is one, and any letters from the executor or the other beneficiaries to hand when you call. Our guide to contesting a will in Victoria explains who is eligible and what the court weighs.

Common questions

Will the estate pay my legal costs if I contest a will?

Only if the court orders it or the parties agree to it in a settlement. A successful claimant's costs are often ordered to be paid from the estate, but that is not guaranteed, and a claimant who loses can be ordered to pay costs.

Do I have to pay a lawyer upfront to contest a will?

That depends on the costs agreement. Some lawyers ask for money on account, and some offer a conditional costs agreement under which some or all of the fees are payable only on a successful outcome. Unless the costs are likely to be modest, the law requires a written estimate of the total legal costs at the start.

Who pays the costs if the claim settles at mediation?

Whatever the settlement says. The terms usually deal with costs, for example by setting an amount for the claimant that includes costs or by providing that each party pays its own, so that part deserves as careful a read as the amount itself.
