What can beneficiaries do when an executor will not distribute the estate?

Wills & Estates 2 Nov 2026 · 8 min read By Spencer Alexander, Principal

A beneficiary can ask the executor for information and accounts at any time and, if that fails, apply to the Supreme Court, which can require an inventory and account and remove an executor who refuses or is unfit to act. An executor cannot be made to distribute within a year of the death, and one who has not applied for probate within six weeks can be called on to explain.

Is there a deadline for an executor to distribute?

Not a fixed one. Section 49 of the Administration and Probate Act 1958 (Vic) says that an executor is not bound to distribute the estate before the end of one year from the death, and that is where the familiar phrase, the executor's year, comes from. People often read it as a promise that the estate will be wound up within twelve months. It works the other way. It protects the executor from being pressed to pay out too early, and it says nothing about when the job must be finished.

What the law expects instead is that an estate is administered with reasonable diligence. A simple estate of a house and a few bank accounts will often be ready to distribute within about a year. An estate with a business, a family trust, a property that has to be sold in a slow market or a claim against it can take considerably longer, and that can be entirely proper. So the useful question is never only how long it has been. It is whether the delay has a reason the executor can explain to you.

What are the legitimate reasons to wait?

Before you conclude that an executor is stalling, it helps to know the reasons that genuinely justify waiting, because several of them are outside the executor's control.

The first is the grant itself. Until probate is granted, banks, share registries and the titles office will generally not let an executor deal with the significant assets, and the application has to be prepared and advertised before it is filed. Our guide to probate in Victoria explains those steps.

The second is the six months after the grant. A person who believes the will did not make adequate provision for them may apply for a family provision order, and under section 99 that application must be made within six months of the grant, although the court can extend the time on an application made before the estate is finally distributed. Section 99A protects an executor who distributes properly after those six months without notice of a claim, which is why a careful executor waits the period out rather than risk paying the estate away and then facing a claim. Our guide to contesting a will explains who can bring one.

The third is the work that follows: selling property, collecting what is owed to the estate, paying the debts, lodging the deceased's final tax return and any estate returns, and sometimes holding money back until the estate's tax position is settled. Each of those steps has its own pace, and an executor who distributes before they are done can end up personally answerable for what is left unpaid.

What is a beneficiary entitled to know?

More than many executors realise. An executor holds the estate for the people entitled under the will, not for themselves, and a beneficiary can expect to be kept properly informed about how that property is being dealt with. In practice that means a copy of the will, which many executors provide early and which becomes part of the court's record once probate is granted, a clear picture of what the estate holds and owes, and, as the administration goes on, accounts showing what has come in, what has been paid out and what remains.

Section 28 of the Act gives this teeth. An executor must, when lawfully required to do so, exhibit on oath in the Supreme Court a true and perfect inventory and account of the estate, and the court keeps its power to require executors to bring in inventories. You do not need to suspect dishonesty to ask for accounts. Wanting to know where the estate stands is reason enough.

What a beneficiary cannot do is run the administration. Within the terms of the will and the law, the executor decides when to sell, which agent to use and how to deal with creditors, and section 48 leaves it to the executor whether to let a beneficiary take possession of land before it is formally transferred. A request to move into the house now, or to take the car early, is one the executor may properly decline.

How should you ask?

In writing, and specifically. A short, civil letter or email can ask for a copy of the will, whether probate has been applied for or granted, a list of the assets and debts, and a statement of what has been received and paid so far. Ask too for the executor's best estimate of when the estate will be distributed, because those answers give a diligent executor everything they need to reassure you. It also gives you a record if they do not answer.

Allow a reasonable time for a reply. Many executors are grieving family members doing the job for the first time, and silence is more often a sign of someone overwhelmed than of anything worse. If the reply is a real answer with a timetable, the problem may already be solved, and our guide to an executor's duties will help you judge whether the timetable is a sensible one. If the executor will not engage, or the answers do not add up, that is the point to take advice.

What if the executor has not applied for probate?

Section 15 deals with this directly. Where the executor named in a will neglects to prove the will or renounce probate within six weeks of the death, or whoever holds the will neglects to bring it into court within that time, any person interested in the estate may apply to the Supreme Court. The court can call on the executor to show cause why they should not prove the will or renounce, or why administration should not be granted to the applicant instead. If the executor does not appear or show sufficient cause, the court can make the orders that are just, including orders about costs, and can grant administration to the applicant.

Six weeks is a short time, and in most families a letter well before any application is the sensible first step. The section matters because it means an executor cannot simply sit on a will. Being named gives a person the right to take on the job or to decline it, not to leave everyone else waiting indefinitely.

When can the court remove an executor?

Once probate has been granted, section 34 allows the Supreme Court to discharge or remove an executor who, after the grant, refuses to act, is unfit to act or is incapable of acting, as well as one who has remained out of Victoria for more than two years or who asks to be discharged. The court can appoint another suitable person or a trustee company as administrator in the executor's place, vest the estate in that person, and make orders about accounts and costs.

Removal is a discretionary order, and the court does not make it lightly. It is not available simply because a beneficiary dislikes the executor or the family has fallen out. The question is whether the executor is refusing, unfit or unable to do the job, and the evidence that tends to raise it is conduct that puts the estate at risk: an executor who will not take the steps the administration needs, who mixes estate money with their own, or who acts for their own benefit rather than the beneficiaries'.

Is court the only way forward?

It is rarely the first step. Many of these disputes end with a lawyer's letter that sets out what the executor must provide and by when, followed by accounts and a timetable the executor then keeps. Where a real disagreement sits behind the delay, between co-executors or over a claim against the estate, mediation is often where it is resolved. A court application comes after that, and its cost needs weighing against what is at stake, because the court decides who bears the costs of the application, and they will not necessarily come out of the estate.

Common questions

Can I insist on my inheritance once twelve months have passed?

Not as of right. Section 49 means an executor cannot be compelled to distribute within the first year, but reaching that mark does not by itself entitle you to be paid. If the estate is still unresolved, ask what remains to be done and when it will be finished, and if there is no proper answer, seek advice about orders.

Can I see the estate's accounts?

You can ask for accounts showing what has come in and what has gone out, and a careful executor will provide them. If they will not, the Supreme Court can require the executor to exhibit an inventory and account of the estate on oath.

Can I make the executor let me live in the house before it is transferred?

Generally not. The Act leaves it to the executor whether to let a beneficiary take possession of land before it is formally transferred, and a person entitled who wants possession or a transfer can ask the court for directions.
