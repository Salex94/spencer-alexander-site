Shareholder agreements: the document that saves business partnerships

Commercial Law 11 May 2026 · 7 min read · updated 9 Nov 2026 Published by Spencer Alexander, Principal

A shareholder agreement is a private contract between a company's owners that sets out how decisions are made, how money flows and how a shareholder leaves. Without one, the company is governed by its constitution and the Corporations Act, rules written for companies in general rather than for yours. It is cheapest and fairest to negotiate while goodwill is high, which is why it is best signed early.

Why is the Corporations Act not enough?

The Act's default rules give a bare majority control of the board and most decisions, and they leave a company owned 50/50 exposed to a deadlock that stops it working. A minority shareholder's main protection is the oppression remedy, a court application that is powerful but slow, public and expensive. Those rules and the company's constitution were written for companies in general rather than for the arrangement between you and your co-owners.

The Australian Securities and Investments Commission sets out on its website what the law requires of every company and its officeholders. A shareholder agreement sits alongside those rules and adds the ones the owners have chosen for their own company.

Who decides what in the company?

The core of the agreement is a clear division of decisions. Day-to-day management stays with the directors, while a defined list of reserved matters needs the agreement of every shareholder or of a set majority, such as borrowing beyond a threshold, issuing shares, selling major assets, employing relatives or changing the nature of the business. That list prevents the common grievance of one owner discovering that the other has committed the company to something large alone. The agreement should also give every shareholder information rights, such as monthly accounts and access to the company's records. Our guide to directors' duties in Victoria explains what the law expects of the directors who make the day-to-day decisions.

How are salaries, dividends and drawings handled?

Disagreements about effort and reward cause many disputes between co-owners, so a good agreement deals with them before they start. It states how working shareholders are paid and how their pay is reviewed, what the dividend policy is, whether profits are distributed or reinvested and in what proportions, and how loans from shareholders are documented and repaid. If one founder works 60 hours a week while the other has stepped back, the agreement should already say how each of them is paid.

How do you break a 50/50 deadlock?

Equal shareholders need an agreed way to break a deadlock, and mediation is the sensible first step. If mediation fails, the agreement can provide for a shotgun clause, under which one shareholder names a price per share and the other must either buy or sell at that price, for an auction between the shareholders, or for an independent valuation with one side leaving. A shotgun clause is fair only between shareholders of similar financial strength, because one who cannot afford to buy is forced to sell, so the right mechanism depends on who the shareholders are and what each of them could fund.

What happens when a shareholder leaves?

Every co-ownership ends eventually, through a sale, retirement, death, disability, insolvency or a falling out, and the agreement sets out in advance what happens in each case. It usually does so through the following provisions.

  • Pre-emptive rights require a departing shareholder to offer their shares to the other shareholders before any outsider.
  • Good leaver and bad leaver terms set a lower price for someone who leaves through misconduct than for a founder who retires at 65.
  • A drag-along clause lets a majority that accepts a genuine offer from a third party require the minority to sell on the same terms, so that the company stays saleable.
  • A tag-along clause lets a minority join a majority's sale on the same terms, so that they are not left in business with a stranger.
  • Buy-sell arrangements on death or total and permanent disability, often funded by insurance, mean that a deceased shareholder's estate is paid out rather than staying on as a shareholder alongside you, which protects both families. How should shares be valued on an exit?

The agreement should fix the method in advance, because almost every exit comes down to a price. An agreement that sets the method, such as a valuation by an independent valuer, an earnings multiple or a set treatment of debts and surplus assets, turns the price into a calculation. An agreement that says nothing leaves the price to be argued, often with a valuer and a lawyer on each side.

How does the agreement protect the business itself?

It binds the shareholders to protect the business they own together. It should include restraints on competing with the company or poaching its staff and clients after an owner leaves, drafted as a series of alternative periods and areas, together with confidentiality obligations and clear ownership of intellectual property, so that the brand, the code or the designs belong to the company rather than to whichever founder happened to create them. For a start-up, founder vesting, under which each founder earns their shares over time, prevents a co-founder who left after four months from owning a third of the company indefinitely.

Two conversations before the drafting begins save the most money. The first is among the owners, about the scenarios that are hardest to raise: one of you wanting out in three years, one falling seriously ill, one losing interest, or a buyer arriving for the whole business. The second is with your accountant, because the funding of a buyout and the tax treatment of a transfer shape which mechanisms are realistic. An agreement drafted after those conversations reflects the company you have, and the owners are far more likely to sign it.

Why sign a shareholder agreement early?

Every co-owned company eventually faces a disagreement, an exit or a death, and an agreement signed while the owners are still on good terms settles the rules for that day before anyone has a reason to argue about them. It also costs a fraction of the dispute it is written to prevent.

Common questions

We are 50/50 and get along well. Do we still need one?

Yes. An agreement is cheapest and fairest to negotiate while goodwill is high, and a company owned 50/50 is the one most likely to reach a deadlock that stops it working.

Is a shareholder agreement the same as the company constitution?

No. The constitution is the company's own set of rules and binds the company, its directors and every shareholder, present and future. A shareholder agreement is a private contract that binds only those who sign it, and it covers matters a constitution handles poorly, such as exits, valuation, restraints and dividends. The two need to be drafted to work together. An agreement cannot change the constitution, which takes a special resolution of the shareholders, so it usually says that it prevails over the constitution between the shareholders who sign it and requires them to vote to change the constitution if the two conflict.

What if my co-owner refuses to sign one?

You cannot force a co-owner to sign, but you can decline to put more money or work into the company on unwritten terms.

Can we add one years after starting?

Yes, and many businesses add one at a funding round or when the business passes to the next generation of a family. The negotiation is harder once the business has value and positions have hardened.
