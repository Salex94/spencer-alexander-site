The contracts every Victorian business should have

Commercial Law 20 Jul 2026 · 8 min read · updated 9 Nov 2026 By Spencer Alexander, Principal

Most Victorian businesses need terms of trade, credit applications with director's guarantees, employment contracts, a shareholder agreement or partnership deed, a reviewed lease, supplier and subcontractor terms and, if they trade online, website terms and a privacy policy. Start with the terms of trade, and register any retention of title on the PPSR within 20 business days of a customer accepting them.

What should your terms of trade cover?

Your terms and conditions govern every sale: when payment falls due, interest and recovery costs on late accounts, who owns goods until payment, a retention of title clause registered on the PPSR to survive a customer's insolvency, limits on your liability, warranty scope consistent with the Australian Consumer Law, and how disputes are handled. That registration is best made as soon as the customer accepts your terms, because where the customer is a company, a registration made more than 20 business days later can be lost if the company goes into liquidation, administration or restructuring within the following six months. Most payment disputes are won or lost on these terms.

Two things make terms enforceable. They must be accepted before the work, on the quote or the credit application rather than on the invoice that follows, and they must match how you actually trade. For most small businesses there is a third, which is fairness. Under the Australian Consumer Law, an unfair term in a standard form contract is void where the customer is an individual buying wholly or mainly for personal, domestic or household use or consumption, or where either party is a small business, meaning one employing fewer than 100 people or with turnover under $10 million in its last income year. Since 9 November 2023, proposing or relying on such a term can itself attract a penalty, so clauses limiting your liability or charging interest and recovery costs need drafting with that test in mind. What should a credit application include?

Credit given to a company needs paperwork of its own. A credit application should record the customer's correct legal identity, which means its ACN and not a trading name, along with trade references, consent to credit checks and a director's personal guarantee. With a guarantee in place, if the company does not pay, you can pursue the director personally for the debt, and that changes the conversation once an account falls overdue. It is also why anyone asked to sign a guarantee should have it reviewed first.

What should every employee sign?

Every employee should have a written contract covering their role, pay, hours, confidentiality, intellectual property, notice and, where the business needs that protection, restraints that apply after they leave. Around the contracts sit the policies that manage risk day to day, such as those on workplace behaviour, leave and IT use. Both need to be kept current, because workplace law has changed quickly in recent years on casual employment, flexible work and contact outside working hours.

Treating employees as contractors remains one of the most expensive mistakes a small business can make, and intentionally underpaying an employee is now a criminal offence. These documents are worth setting up properly and reviewing regularly.

What agreements do co-owners need?

If your company has more than one shareholder, a shareholder agreement is usually the most important document you can sign, because it settles how decisions are made, how a deadlock is broken, how dividends are paid and how an owner leaves and at what value. Partnerships need the equivalent partnership deed, and family trusts that trade need deeds that someone has actually read. Many of the disputes that end businesses begin between co-owners who never agreed in writing how decisions are made and how one of them can leave. Why have a lease reviewed before you sign?

Your lease is likely your largest fixed commitment, and if your use is retail, Victoria's Retail Leases Act gives you protections worth knowing before signature: disclosure statements, outgoings rules, the ban on charging you the landlord's land tax or commercial and industrial property tax, and the minimum five year term. Never sign a lease, a renewal or an assignment without having it reviewed. Why do supplier and subcontractor agreements matter?

Your contracts with suppliers deserve the same attention as your contracts with customers. They should deal with delivery obligations and what happens when a critical supplier fails, how prices can be reviewed, who owns the intellectual property in anything designed for you, the insurance and safety obligations of subcontractors, and your right to end a failing relationship cleanly. A business that documents only its sales often finds out in its first supply problem how exposed it was to its suppliers.

What does trading online add?

Trading online adds website terms of use, a privacy policy that reflects what you collect and do with personal information and, for services, engagement terms that clients accept before the work begins. Privacy law has been strengthened in recent years, so the privacy policy needs to be accurate rather than a formality, and if your revenue depends on subscriptions, platforms or data, these documents are part of what you sell.

From 1 July 2027 the Australian Consumer Law will also prohibit unfair trading practices towards consumers, require certain charges payable with each purchase to be shown close to an advertised price, and add new rules for subscription contracts, so terms written now for consumer sales or subscriptions should be drafted with those changes in view.

Which missing contracts cause the most disputes?

In our disputes work, the documents most often missing are the ones between people who trusted each other: two founders who never signed a shareholder agreement, a supplier relationship that ran for eight years on a single email, or a senior employee who left with the client list because the contract never mentioned one. Good terms protect that trust, because they settle the hard questions while everyone still gets along.

How often should you review your business contracts?

Review them once a year, and sooner if the business changes. Prices move, the Australian Consumer Law is amended, court decisions change how restraints are best drafted and the business itself grows, so a light annual review, much like your insurance renewal, keeps the set current and costs far less than the first dispute it prevents. What difference do the right documents make?

Consider a Melbourne trades business with one client worth a third of its revenue, engaged on the client's own purchase orders, which impose 90-day payment terms and unlimited liability. New terms of trade, a credit application with a director's guarantee and a registration on the PPSR could all be in place within a fortnight. Suppose that eight months later the client's group restructures amid insolvency. With registered terms and a guarantee in place, that business would be far better placed than the unsecured suppliers waiting on a dividend, although none of that paperwork would have felt urgent in the eight months before it was needed.

When should a business put its contracts in place?

Put them in place before they feel necessary, because that is when they cost the least and are easiest to agree. Put in writing the relationships that carry your revenue, your equity and your premises, review the set each year, and have each document drafted by lawyers who have seen its clauses tested in disputes.

Common questions

Which contract should a business put in place first?

Start with your terms of trade, which protect your revenue, then a shareholder agreement, which protects your equity, and then the lease and your staff. For most businesses, those first two or three documents cover the relationships in which disputes most often arise.

When do terms of trade need to be accepted?

They need to be accepted before the work begins, which in practice means on the quote or the credit application rather than on the invoice that follows, and they must also match how you actually trade.

Do I need a shareholder agreement if my company has more than one owner?

In practice, yes. A shareholder agreement settles how decisions are made, how a deadlock is broken, how dividends are paid and how an owner leaves and at what value. Many of the disputes that end businesses begin between co-owners who never agreed in writing how decisions are made and how one of them can leave.

Should I sign a director's personal guarantee?

Do not sign one until you understand what it exposes you to. A personal guarantee makes you liable for what the company owes that creditor if the company does not pay, which puts your own assets, including your home, within reach, and many guarantees continue for as long as the company keeps trading with that creditor. Have it reviewed before you sign.
