We agree on the children. Do we need a lawyer for consent orders?

Family Law 9 Nov 2026 · 8 min read Published by Spencer Alexander, Principal

Not by law, but it is usually worth it. A parenting plan you both sign records what you have agreed, but it cannot be enforced the way court orders can. Consent orders can, and are usually made without either of you attending court. A lawyer's real work is making the orders precise enough to last, and filing the signed application within 90 days, before the other parent's consent lapses.

Does the law require a lawyer for consent orders?

No. Parents can apply for consent orders themselves, and the court's rules allow the draft orders to be signed by each parent personally or by their lawyer. Where the arrangements are simple and both of you understand exactly what you are signing, that can work.

The reason most parents still bring in a lawyer has little to do with the form and a great deal to do with the words. Consent orders are the document a school or a judge will read years from now, perhaps when the goodwill has gone, and an order that seemed obvious at the kitchen table can turn out to mean two different things to two different parents.

Is a parenting plan enough?

Sometimes, but you should know what it is and what it is not. Under the Family Law Act 1975 (Cth), a parenting plan is a written agreement between the parents, signed and dated by both of you and made free from any threat, duress or coercion. It can cover where the children live, the time they spend with each of you, how you communicate and how major decisions are made, and you can change it whenever you both agree in writing.

What a parenting plan cannot do is be enforced the way an order can. The Act itself notes that parents who want enforceable arrangements need court orders, and that those orders can be made by consent. A plan still counts for something: if a court is later asked to make parenting orders, it must have regard to the most recent plan where that is in the children's best interests. A plan suits parents who cooperate well and want flexibility; consent orders suit parents who want certainty, or who expect the arrangements to come under strain.

How do consent orders work?

Where there is no existing court case, you file an Application for Consent Orders in the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia, together with the orders you have agreed, signed by both of you. The requirement that most parents attempt family dispute resolution before applying for parenting orders is one the court can excuse where everyone consents to the orders sought. A registrar or judge then considers the application, usually without either of you attending court, and can make the orders, ask either of you for more information, or dismiss the application.

Agreement does not take the decision out of the court's hands. The Act requires the court to treat the children's best interests as the paramount consideration in deciding whether to make any parenting order, consent orders included, although it allows the court to decide how far to examine each of the matters it would weigh at a trial. The rules also require each of you to tell the court whether you consider a child has been, or is at risk of being, subjected or exposed to abuse, neglect or family violence, and whether either of you has been or is at risk of being subjected to family violence. Where there is such a concern, you must explain how the proposed orders deal with it. Once made, consent orders have the same force as orders made by a judge after a hearing.

One timing rule catches people out. The responding parent's consent lapses if the application is not filed within 90 days of the date it was first signed, so an application that sits in a drawer while one of you has second thoughts may need to be signed again.

What does a lawyer check before you sign?

Most of the work is turning an arrangement you understand into words that only mean one thing.

The first check is precision. "Alternate weekends" needs a start date, a pick up time and a place, school holidays need a rotation, and Christmas, birthdays and the day a changeover falls on a public holiday all need a rule that sits above the regular pattern. Every gap is a future argument.

The second is decision making. Orders can give you joint decision making about the major long term issues in a child's life, which the Act defines to include education, religious and cultural upbringing, health, the child's name, and a move that would make it significantly harder for a child to spend time with a parent. Joint decision making means each of you must consult the other and make a genuine effort to agree, while the parent the children are with need not consult the other about day to day matters, unless the orders say otherwise. The orders can also set out the steps to take before either of you goes back to court, including returning to a family dispute resolution practitioner, and a lawyer makes sure they do.

The third is the interaction with later agreements, which surprises most parents. Unless the court orders otherwise in exceptional circumstances, parenting orders are taken to be subject to any parenting plan the two of you sign afterwards. That gives you room to adjust the arrangements as the children grow without filing anything, but it also means a hastily signed note can change court orders.

The fourth is what the orders leave out. Parenting orders do not generally deal with child support, which is assessed under its own legislation, so the money needs separate attention, and our guide to how child support is calculated explains how that works. If you are also dividing property, those orders can be prepared at the same time, which our guide to making an agreed property settlement final covers.

What if the arrangements need to change later?

Children's needs change, and the orders that suit a toddler rarely suit a teenager. If you both agree, you can change the arrangements by a new parenting plan, subject to the point above, or apply for new consent orders. If you do not agree, the Act makes it deliberately harder to reopen final orders: before reconsidering them, the court must consider whether there has been a significant change of circumstances and be satisfied that reconsidering them is in the children's best interests, although it can do so with the agreement of everyone bound by the orders. That is a good reason to get the orders right the first time, and to build in a way of reviewing them at sensible points, such as the start of secondary school.

Parenting orders stop being in force when a child turns 18, marries or enters a de facto relationship.

Can one lawyer act for both of us?

In practice, no. Even when you agree, each of you has your own interests in how the orders are worded, so a lawyer advising on them acts for one parent only. One parent usually engages a lawyer to prepare the application and the proposed orders, and the other either takes their own advice or signs without a lawyer. Common questions

Do we have to go to court to get consent orders?

Usually not. An Application for Consent Orders is normally dealt with without either of you attending court, and the court can make the orders, ask for more information or dismiss the application.

Do we need a mediation certificate for consent orders?

Most applications for parenting orders need a family dispute resolution certificate, but the court can grant an exemption where all parties consent to the orders sought.

Can a parenting plan change our consent orders?

Usually, yes. Parenting orders are taken to be subject to a later parenting plan signed by both parents, unless the court ordered otherwise in exceptional circumstances.

Is there a deadline for applying for consent orders about the children?

The Family Law Act sets no deadline for parenting orders, but no parenting order can be made for a child who is 18 or over, and the other parent's consent to an unfiled application lapses 90 days after it was first signed if it has not been filed.
