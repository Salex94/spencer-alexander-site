Testamentary trusts: protecting an inheritance in Victoria

Wills & Estates 25 May 2026 · 6 min read · updated 9 Nov 2026 Published by Spencer Alexander, Principal

A testamentary trust is a trust your will creates when you die, so a trustee holds the inheritance rather than the beneficiary receiving it outright. Properly drafted, it generally keeps the money outside a bankrupt beneficiary's personal estate, gives partial protection if a relationship breaks down, and lets children under 18 receive income from inherited assets taxed at ordinary adult rates. Not everyone needs one.

What is a testamentary trust?

A testamentary trust is a trust created by your will, made under the Wills Act 1997 (Vic), and it comes into existence when you die. Instead of passing directly to a beneficiary, the inheritance passes to a trustee, often the beneficiary themselves, who holds and manages it for a group of potential beneficiaries, typically that person and their spouse, children and grandchildren. The most common form is discretionary, which means the trustee decides each year how income and capital are applied within the family group. Your primary beneficiary can usually control the trust and benefit from it, although control can weaken its protection in a family law property dispute.

How does a testamentary trust protect an inheritance?

Assets held in a properly drafted testamentary trust are owned by the trustee, not by your beneficiary personally, and that distinction matters when something goes wrong in the beneficiary's life.

If your adult child runs a business or gives personal guarantees, an inheritance received outright is exposed to their creditors. Held in a discretionary testamentary trust, it generally sits outside their personal estate.

If their relationship breaks down, an inheritance received outright joins the property pool on separation. Trust assets are treated differently, but the family courts can still take a trust into account, particularly where the beneficiary controls it, so the protection is partial. Since the June 2025 property reforms the courts' approach remains case by case, as our note on the 2025 family law changes explains.

For a child with a disability, an addiction or exposure to financial exploitation, a trust lets a trustee manage the money rather than handing it over outright, and in some cases a special disability trust or a protective structure with an independent trustee is the better design.

How does a testamentary trust reduce tax?

Income a testamentary trust distributes to children under 18 is taxed at ordinary adult rates, with the tax-free threshold, whereas minors who receive distributions from an ordinary family trust are taxed at penalty rates above a small threshold. Generally, the concession applies only to income from assets that came to the trust from the deceased estate, and from what those assets earn or are reinvested into, so money added to the trust from other sources does not usually qualify. For a family where an inheritance produces investment income while the children are young, to pay school fees for instance, the difference can amount to thousands of dollars a year.

In the May 2026 Budget the Australian Government announced a minimum tax of 30 per cent on the income of discretionary trusts from 1 July 2028, and the bills for it were introduced into Federal Parliament in October 2026. The Government has said that deceased estates and discretionary testamentary trusts established for genuine testamentary purposes will be excluded, but the exclusion the bills give a testamentary trust is narrower than that. Broadly, it covers only income from property that came from the deceased estate, and for a trust established on or after 1 July 2028 it also depends on who the will allows the trust to benefit, so a will that lets the trust benefit a family company or another trust may need different drafting. The bills are not yet law and may change before they pass, so anyone weighing the tax treatment of a testamentary trust should take current advice. The Australian Taxation Office website sets out how deceased estates and testamentary trusts are taxed today.

How can a testamentary trust help a blended family?

A testamentary trust can provide for a current partner while preserving capital for your own children, which no outright gift can do. The trust can give your partner an income or a home for life, with the capital passing to your children afterwards. Rather than relying on a surviving partner's new will to look after your children, the trust sets out in your own will who receives the capital after your partner's death. It also reduces the pressure that so often produces estate disputes in blended families.

What does a testamentary trust cost?

A will with a testamentary trust costs more to prepare than a simple will, because the drafting is more complex. It starts from $1,650 including GST for one person, and wills with testamentary trusts for a couple start from $3,025. A trust that is activated after your death also has modest ongoing costs, including a tax file number, annual tax returns and the trustee's record-keeping. Two design points keep this proportionate. A trust can be optional, so that your executor or beneficiary decides after your death whether to use it or take the gift outright, judged against the circumstances at the time, and thresholds can be set so that small inheritances pass outright and only substantial ones are held on trust.

Who does not need a testamentary trust?

Not everyone needs one. If your estate is modest, your beneficiaries are financially stable adults and no one faces creditor or relationship risk, a well-drafted simple will may serve perfectly well, and a single will is a fixed fee of $495 including GST. The aim is a will that matches the risks your family actually faces.

Who should be the trustee?

Many people appoint the primary beneficiary as their own trustee, preserving independence and control. Where protection is the point, for a vulnerable beneficiary or one at risk of spending the inheritance unwisely, an independent or joint trustee is the wiser choice. The will should also deal with who replaces a trustee who dies or stops acting and, in larger structures, appoint an appointor who can replace a trustee, because a testamentary trust may run for decades.

How does a testamentary trust run each year?

In practice a testamentary trust is simple to run. The trustee opens a bank account for the trust, invests the inheritance, and each year decides, usually with an accountant, how the income is distributed among the family group before 30 June. Records are kept, a tax return is lodged and the beneficiaries receive statements. For a straightforward investment portfolio this is a few hours of administration a year, and run properly, the trust works much like a family investment account with the added protections of a trust.

Common questions

Can my child collapse the trust and take the money?

If they control the trust as trustee, generally yes, because most testamentary trusts are designed to be flexible. Where you want real restraint, the will can appoint an independent trustee, release capital in stages, or both.

Does the trust protect against every family court claim?

No. The family courts can treat a trust that a beneficiary controls as that person's financial resource, and in some cases as their property. The protection is real, but it is stronger where control is shared or held by an independent trustee.

What happens when the trust ends?

A trust must vest within the maximum period the law allows, and in Victoria a will can set that period at up to 80 years, which is long enough to cover grandchildren. On vesting the capital is distributed, and any tax consequences are worked through with advice at the time.
