Who we act for

When businesses call us about a debt.

We act for businesses owed money by customers, clients and other businesses, from a single overdue invoice to a larger account in dispute, and for companies and their directors when a statutory demand or a court claim arrives. People usually call when reminders have been ignored, promises to pay have not been kept, or a customer has gone quiet, and acting early usually keeps more options open.

The documents behind the debt matter most: your terms of trade, the quote or contract, delivery records and the invoices. Our guide to recovering unpaid debts in Victoria explains each step in more detail.

How we help

What we do to recover a debt.

Letters of demand

A letter on our letterhead to the debtor, setting out what is owed, any interest your contract allows and a deadline to pay. For an undisputed debt it often resolves the matter, or opens the way to a payment plan that we can put in writing for you.

Court proceedings

If the debt is not paid, we issue proceedings in the court that fits the amount. The Magistrates' Court can hear a claim for a debt of up to $100,000, which covers most unpaid invoices, and larger claims are usually brought in the County Court or the Supreme Court. Once you have judgment, we advise on enforcing it.

Statutory demands

For a company debtor, we prepare and serve a statutory demand where the debt is due and payable, at least $4,000 and not genuinely disputed. Unless the debt is a judgment debt, the demand must come with an affidavit verifying that the debt is due and payable.

When your company is served

We check the demand and the debt the day it arrives, and advise whether to pay, secure or compromise it or apply to set it aside. The court can set a demand aside, or reduce it, where there is a genuine dispute about the existence or amount of the debt or the company has an offsetting claim, and can set it aside where a defect in it would otherwise cause substantial injustice or there is some other reason it should be set aside.

Time limits

The deadlines in debt recovery.

For a creditor, the main limit is six years to sue on a debt under an ordinary contract, counted from when the right to sue arose, usually when payment fell due. A written acknowledgment of the debt signed by the debtor, or a part payment, starts the six years again, so an older debt may still be recoverable.

A company served with a statutory demand has 21 days from service, and an application to set it aside, with its supporting affidavit, must be filed and served on the creditor within those 21 days. That deadline cannot be extended. If the company neither complies nor applies in time, a court hearing a winding up application made within the following three months, by that creditor or anyone else, must presume that the company is insolvent unless the contrary is proved. Good to know

Common questions about debt recovery.

What should a company do when it is served with a statutory demand?

Act the same day you receive it. A company served with a statutory demand has 21 days to pay the debt, to secure it or reach a compromise to the creditor's reasonable satisfaction, or to apply to court to set the demand aside, and the application and its supporting affidavit must be both filed and served on the creditor within those 21 days. That deadline cannot be extended. If the company does none of these, and an application to wind it up is made within the following three months, by that creditor or anyone else, the court must presume the company is insolvent unless the contrary is proved. Call us on (03) 9125 8355 the day it arrives.

Should we serve a statutory demand or sue?

A statutory demand suits a company debt that is due and payable, at least $4,000 and not genuinely disputed, because it puts the onus on the company to act within 21 days. Where the debt is genuinely disputed or the company has an offsetting claim, the court can set the demand aside or reduce it, and where it sets the demand aside it can order you to pay the company's costs, so a court claim is usually the better course.

How long do we have to recover a debt?

A claim for a debt under an ordinary contract generally has to be brought within six years after the right to sue arose, usually when payment fell due. A written acknowledgment of the debt signed by the debtor, or a part payment, starts the six years again. Acting early is still wise, because a debtor's position tends to worsen with time.

What if the person who owes us is an individual, not a company?

A statutory demand is served on a company. Against an individual, such as a sole trader or a guarantor, you generally need a judgment first. A final judgment or order for at least $10,000, made within the last six years, can support a bankruptcy notice, and a debtor served in Australia who neither complies within 21 days nor satisfies the court of a counter-claim, set-off or cross demand that meets the Act's test commits an act of bankruptcy, which can found a creditor's petition. Bankruptcy is a serious step for both sides, and we advise on whether it is worth taking.
