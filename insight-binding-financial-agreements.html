Binding financial agreements in Australia: prenups that actually hold

Family Law 27 Apr 2026 · 6 min read · updated 9 Nov 2026 Published by Spencer Alexander, Principal

A binding financial agreement, often called a prenup, is a private contract between married or de facto partners that settles how property and superannuation would be divided on separation. It generally holds only if you each get independent advice from separate lawyers before signing, and courts can still set one aside for non-disclosure, pressure or changed circumstances. We draft and review them, and advise when consent orders fit better.

What is a binding financial agreement?

A binding financial agreement, or BFA, is a private contract between partners, married or de facto, that deals with how property, superannuation and financial resources would be divided on separation, and can also deal with spousal maintenance. A properly made agreement excludes the court's power to divide your property under the usual just and equitable test, so you are choosing your own rules in place of the court's. The Family Law Act 1975 (Cth), under which these agreements are made, surrounds them with strict formalities for exactly that reason.

When can you make a binding financial agreement?

You can make one before you marry or move in together, which is the classic prenup, often protecting assets built up before the relationship, an expected inheritance or what you intend for children from an earlier relationship. You can make one during the relationship, commonly when circumstances change, such as when a business is founded, an inheritance arrives or a couple reconciles on agreed financial terms. You can also make one after separation or divorce, as one of the two ways to finalise a property settlement without going to court. De facto couples, including same-sex couples, have their own parallel provisions in the Act, and the timing changes how the agreement is drafted rather than how it works.

What makes a financial agreement binding?

For a BFA to bind, each party must receive independent legal advice, from separate lawyers, about the effect of the agreement on their rights and its advantages and disadvantages, before signing. Each lawyer signs a statement confirming the advice; the statements are exchanged; the agreement is signed by both parties. Skip or shortcut any element and the agreement is generally not binding. A court has a limited power to declare an agreement binding where the advice or statement requirements were not met and it would be unjust and inequitable to treat it as not binding, but that is a discretionary rescue no one should plan to rely on. This is not a document you can buy as a template, sign at the kitchen table and expect to hold a decade later against a well-advised challenge.

When can a court set a BFA aside?

Courts can, and do, set aside financial agreements. The recognised grounds include fraud and non-disclosure of significant assets; the agreement being signed under duress, undue influence or unconscionable pressure, of which the wedding-eve ultimatum is the notorious example; impracticability arising from changed circumstances; and a material change concerning the care of a child that would cause hardship. None of this makes an agreement futile, but it does mean an agreement must be prepared without haste, on full disclosure, with independent advice on both sides, and reviewed when life changes.

BFA or consent orders?

For a separated couple who agree, consent orders are usually the simpler route. They are made by the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia, which checks that the division is just and equitable, and once made they carry the court's authority and are very hard to disturb. A BFA involves no court and no fairness check, which makes it faster and more private, and it suits arrangements a court might not approve, or couples who have not separated at all, for whom consent orders are generally not available. BFAs are therefore most useful before and during a relationship, and in unusual situations after one. What should a binding financial agreement cover?

  • A complete, honest schedule of each party's assets, liabilities and super, because full disclosure is what the rest of the agreement depends on.
  • How existing assets are quarantined, and how property acquired together is shared.
  • Treatment of the home, businesses, trusts and inheritances, both received and expected.
  • Superannuation, including any splitting arrangements.
  • Spousal maintenance, to the extent the law allows it to be dealt with.
  • Review triggers, such as marriage, the birth of a child or the passing of 10 years. For de facto couples, marriage is more than a review trigger. An agreement made under the de facto provisions stops being binding if the couple later marry each other, so a couple who may marry should have the agreement drafted so that it also operates as an agreement made in contemplation of marriage. An agreement written for a two-year relationship rarely suits one that has lasted 20 years.

How do you raise a prenup with your partner?

Raising a prenup with someone you love can feel uncomfortable. Couples who handle it well treat it as financial openness rather than distrust: both partners see everything, both get independent advice and both know where they stand. Started months rather than weeks before a wedding, it becomes one honest conversation instead of a standing grievance.

Common questions

Will asking for a prenup damage our relationship?

Not if it is raised early and handled openly. Couples who find the conversation hard often find money hard to talk about generally, and the agreement puts the full financial picture on the table once, early, with advisers involved, which is a healthier foundation than discovering each other's debts years later.

Can one lawyer act for both of us to save money?

No. The Act requires each of you to receive independent legal advice before signing, and one lawyer cannot advise both of you independently, so an agreement made that way is generally not binding.

My partner handed me an agreement two weeks before the wedding. Now what?

Slow everything down. Signing under that kind of pressure is precisely what courts examine when agreements are challenged. A proper review takes time; a wedding date is not a legal deadline.

We signed one years ago and never updated it. Does it still work?

Perhaps, but children, business sales and property purchases can strain an old agreement's assumptions, and a material change in circumstances relating to the care, welfare and development of a child that would cause hardship is a statutory ground for setting agreements aside, so have the agreement reviewed whenever your circumstances change.
